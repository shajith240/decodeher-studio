When I went through 52 million posts across 10 platforms for our State of Social Media Engagement report , LinkedIn had the highest median engagement rate of any platform we looked at, at around 6.2%. LinkedIn has also become the most-cited website in AI search for professional questions, according to Profound's tracking, and more of what gets cited is what people post. So what you share on LinkedIn can show up when someone asks ChatGPT about your field. It's worked for me, too. I made my account in 2017 but only started posting in 2020 , when I was looking for community after losing my job. You don't need millions of followers, and you don't need to be the world's foremost expert in your niche. A smaller following that cares about what you have to say will do far more for you than a huge one that doesn't. So if you really, really want to grow on LinkedIn, whether as a creator, a founder, a business owner, or someone starting from zero, this guide is for you. Switch your profile button from Connect to Follow This is the first, easiest thing anyone can do to objectively “get more followers.” By default, the big button on your LinkedIn profile says Connect. When you switch it to Follow, anyone can follow you in one tap instead of sending a request and waiting for you to accept it. Here's how followers and connections compare: Connections are capped at 30,000 and need a yes from both sides. Followers have no cap, and anyone can follow you without waiting on you. And you won't lose anything by switching, since people can still send you a connection request from the More menu on your profile. LinkedIn used to bundle this setting into Creator Mode, which it retired in March 2024, but the setting still exists on its own. To turn it on, go to Settings & Privacy → Visibility → Visibility of your LinkedIn activity → Followers , and turn on "Make follow primary." 2. Write a LinkedIn headline that says who you are and who you help Your LinkedIn headline shows up next to your name everywhere: on your posts, in your comments, and in search results. Most people use it for their job title, but unless you work in a role like Chief Imagination Officer or you're the CEO of a Fortune 100 company, your title alone likely isn't enough reason for someone to follow you. Mine is "senior content creator @ buffer | content about my creator career." That's the simplest formula: your role plus what you post about. The first half is about me, and the second half tells you what you'll get if you follow. Carmen Vicente leads with "Social Strategist in tech," then follows with LinkedIn Top Voice, 40k on TikTok, and B2B Social's Rising 30. Erifili Gounari does a longer version of the same thing. What you do for who + your credentials . Brandon Smithwrick 's starts with "I teach content playbooks for marketers & creators" and then lists his newsletter, Forbes 30U30, and the companies he's worked for. You'll see "I help…" used the same way on a lot of consultant profiles. Your title + your brand . Sophie Miller 's is just "creator + founder @prettylittlemarketer". At 242,000 followers, people already know the brand, so she doesn’t need to do much more explaining. Whichever formula you use, put the most important part first. You get 220 characters, but only the first 60 to 70 show up in search results and comment sections. Use your LinkedIn About section to get people invested in you Your About section is the one place on your profile where you have real room to chat, with 2,600 characters to play with. I've seen many people use that space to paste what amounts to a CV, but anyone reading it has already seen your headline and job title, and your Experience section covers the rest. What visitors don't know yet is who you (really) are. By the time someone reaches your About section, they've probably seen one of your posts and your headline, and they're deciding whether they want to keep seeing more. So this is the place to share some of your story, career-wise and content-wise, and give them a reason to care. A few things worth including: How you got to where you are in your career/life, especially if it wasn't a straight line. What you make content about, and why it matters to you. What you'd like people to do next, whether that's follow you, subscribe to something, or get in touch. And like your headline, put the important part first. Only about the first 300 characters show before "…see more," and plenty of people won't click it. My LinkedIn About section Here's how four people I follow handle theirs. Two are creators, one runs an agency, and one consults for brands, and they're all quite different: Carmen Vicente goes all in on who she is: two paragraphs about how she thinks and what she cares about, with no credentials and no call to action. Her headline already carries the Top Voice badge and follower counts, so her About can focus on personality. Brandon Smithwrick tells his story in short lines. He includes where he's worked, what he teaches now, his credentials in one "TLDR" paragraph, and then two arrows at the bottom, one to subscribe and one that says "Or just hit follow. Rachel Karten writes three plain paragraphs: who she is and her newsletter, who she's worked with and where, and "Want to work with me? Shoot an email over to…". 4. Put your best work in the Featured section The Featured section sits just below your About section, so it's often the first place people see your actual content. You can pin posts, articles (including newsletter editions), links to things like your website, and media like PDFs or slides. This is the one place on your profile where you decide what someone sees first, so choose what you'd most want them to see before they follow you. Your best-performing post, whatever the format. It's already shown that people like it. Your newsletter, lead magnet, or booking link, if you have one. It's the easiest place on your profile to turn a visitor into a subscriber or a client. Create LinkedIn content people follow you for Once your profile is sorted, your content is what actually gets people to hit Follow, because it's how most people will find you in the first place. LinkedIn only shows your posts to people it thinks will engage with them, the same way your own feed only shows you posts it thinks you'll stop for. So everything in this section comes back to two things: helping LinkedIn work out who your posts are for, and making those posts worth stopping for. 🎥 If you'd rather watch, my colleague Kirsti Lang walks through the LinkedIn playbook that got her to 12K followers and 6 million impressions. 5. Understand what the LinkedIn algorithm looks for LinkedIn's feed tries to predict two things about every post: whether people will stay on it, and whether they'll react to it. In a paper LinkedIn's engineers published in early 2026 about how the feed ranks posts, they call the first one "long dwell" (staying on a post past a certain point). The second is whether someone likes, comments on, or shares it. LinkedIn also announced a rebuilt feed in March 2026 that uses AI to understand what a post is about, and updates its picture of what each person is interested in within minutes. If you've ever liked a couple of posts on a topic and then seen nothing but that topic for a week, that's what's going on. LinkedIn doesn't publish how it weights any of this, so no one can tell you the exact number of seconds someone needs to spend on your post. But in practice, it's fairly simple: If you post about the same few things consistently, LinkedIn can more easily work out who to show your posts to. If every post is about something completely different, that's harder. And once a post reaches the right people, it has to hold their attention and give them a reason to react. 📚 For more on how LinkedIn decides what to show, including what the LinkedIn team told us directly, read our full breakdown of how the LinkedIn algorithm works . 6. Niche down to 3 to 5 topics you could talk about for hours If LinkedIn's feed is trying to work out who to show your posts to, the easiest thing you can do is make that obvious by picking a small number of topics and sticking with them. Now, as someone who rages against the niche machine, I understand if you immediately want to scroll past this tip. But WAIT! Give me a second. If you've already found the overlap between what you want to make content about and what people want to learn from you, you can skip this one. But if you're still getting to know your audience or figuring out what you want to create (or both), starting niche and then going broad might work better for you. You can probably name three topics you could spend hours talking about, and you don't have to be an expert in any of them. If you're job searching, that's a topic. If you're running a business, what you're figuring out along the way (pricing, hiring, landing your first big client) is a topic. Learning a new skill counts too, because you'll see it differently than someone with 10 years of experience. Then think about who you want to reach and where they get stuck. Your topics sit where that overlaps with what you know. If you're stuck, picture a podcast you'd get invited on and ask yourself what the host would want you to talk about. The narrower you can get, the better. "Marketing" is too broad. "B2B SaaS marketing for early-stage founders" is a niche people will see themselves in and follow you for. If I had to pick three pillars, I'd say mine are content creation, remote work, and the business of being online. You can see all three reflected in my About section, and most of what I post lands in one of them. (I do wander off into photography and lifestyle content now and then, but the core three don't change too much.) My colleague Hailley Griffis, who leads communications at Buffer, works the same way with three pillars of her own: her career and work at Buffer, the systems she uses in work and life, and brand marketing. She grew her LinkedIn audience by 26% in the first half of 2025 with them. A focused following can also go further than a bigger one. As LinkedIn consultant Melanie Goodman put it this year , "An account with 8,000 focused followers can outperform one with 80,000 unfocused ones." 7. Write LinkedIn hooks that make people click "See more” Your hook is the opening of your post, and its job is to get people to click "…see more" (and, ideally, hit Follow while they're at it). You don't get much room to do it, either. Only the first line or two shows in the feed before the cutoff, so whatever makes someone stop has to be right at the top. In AuthoredUp's analysis of nearly 310,000 LinkedIn posts from December 2025 to May 2026, four kinds of opening lines did best. Here they are, in order: Start in the middle of a story. "I got fired on a Tuesday. By Friday I had three job offers." Say the opposite of what people expect. "The best time to post on LinkedIn isn't first thing in the morning." Make a bold claim, but only if you have the data to back it up. "Posting more on LinkedIn doesn't hurt your reach. We checked 2 million posts." Lead with a number or a result. "My impressions went up 122% in seven days. Here's what changed." You'll notice questions aren't on that list. They're the most common hook advice out there, but they came last in AuthoredUp's study, with a median engagement rate of 2.16% against 2.60% for story openers. That matches what I've seen on my own posts, though I'd take any single study on this with a pinch of salt. Use this test on your next post: if you saw only the first two lines from a stranger, would you click "…see more"? 📚 I've written more about the anatomy of a great hook if you want to go deeper. 8. Post like a person (and be careful with AI) Once someone's clicked "…see more," the post has to give them a reason to stay and a reason to react. The simplest way I've found is to post the way I'd talk to a friend or colleague about whatever I have going on. I said in the intro that you don't need to be the world's foremost expert, and this is where that matters most. Looking back at my best posts from the last few months, most fall into two groups: Something you tried, and what actually happened. My 30 days of posting daily and my Build Week project both got written up this way, and the Build Week post is one of my most engaged recent posts. Something you're figuring out as it happens. My cousin asked me what my job actually involves, I wrote it out for her, and that one post turned into a series (Career Creator Couch) that's now some of my best-performing content. None of these needed special expertise. I just paid attention to what was going on in my life and shared it in a way other people could relate to. It works the same way if you run a business: a decision you made, a client project that went sideways, something you're testing right now. I know a lot of people use AI to write their LinkedIn posts now, and I've been there too. In November 2023, I published nothing but AI-written posts for a week , with only a quick proofread, and nobody noticed. My impressions were up 11%, and my engagement was up 75% compared with a typical week. At the time, I wrote that if you'd built up trust with your audience, letting AI take a swing "seems like a no-brainer." I wouldn't run that experiment the same way today. In July 2026, LinkedIn added a "Seems like AI slop" option to the report menu on every post, and more than a million people used it in the first two weeks. Flagged content is seeing about 40% fewer views. LinkedIn also retired its own "Enhance your post" AI writing feature and replaced it with a plain proofreader. Separately, Originality.AI found that about 81% of the long-form LinkedIn posts it sampled in July 2026 read as likely AI, up from about half in late 2024. Whatever you make of AI detectors, LinkedIn has clearly decided this is a problem, and so have readers. So my position now is somewhere in the middle. I’ll still use AI to suggest hook variations, tidy a draft, and tell me what might be useful to add. But the post has to start from something rooted in my own experience, and it has to end up sounding like me. ⚡ Pro tip: If you want help with hook variations, Buffer's AI Assistant can suggest some for any post you draft, and you decide what stays. Get it for free → 9. Keep people on LinkedIn You need to be making zero-click content. That's a post where all the context is in the post itself, so nobody has to click away to get it. I know a lot of us are also trying to get people somewhere else, whether that's a newsletter, a YouTube video, or a company website, and the temptation to describe it and drop a link in is real. LinkedIn says there's no penalty for links. Rishi Jobanputra , a senior director of product management at LinkedIn, said so in September 2025. But Buffer's own data says link posts do reach fewer people. We looked at a year of LinkedIn posts from more than 58,000 accounts that post through Buffer, comparing posts with and without links from the same account in the same month. Posts with a link preview got 28% fewer impressions and 44% fewer engagements than posts without a link. Posts with a URL in the text had a smaller drop: about 16% fewer impressions and 16% fewer engagements. Because both of those numbers dropped by the same amount, the engagement rate on posts with a URL in the text didn't change. So people engaged with those posts as much as usual when they saw them. LinkedIn just showed them to fewer people. ⚡️ Link previews did get about 87% more clicks, as LinkedIn counts them, so if a click matters more than reach for a particular post, that trade can be worth it. I think it comes back to the LinkedIn algorithm focus of “long dwell". A link sends people off LinkedIn, and the feed prefers posts people stay on, so link posts can lose reach without LinkedIn penalizing them on purpose. So, what to do about it: Put the full value in the post. If the newsletter has three points, the post can have all three, and the link is for people who want the longer version. Or put it in the comment. In our data, posts where the account added the link in its own comment got about 15% more impressions than similar posts with no link at all. (That's only from accounts whose comments we can see through Buffer, so it's more a promising sign than proof.) If you use Buffer, you can schedule that first comment to go out with your post on paid plans. 📚 We've got a full guide to zero-click content if you want to go deeper. 10. Try different post formats, starting with carousels LinkedIn gives you a handful of formats to post, and they perform very differently. When I went through 52 million posts for our State of Social Media Engagement report , here's how the main ones compared on LinkedIn: Format Median engagement rate Carousels (document posts) 21.77% Video 7.35% Images 6.52% Link posts 3.81% Text-only 3.18% Carousels are the standout, with a median engagement rate about three times video's and nearly seven times text-only posts'. A carousel on LinkedIn is a PDF you upload as a document post, which shows up as slides people swipe through. They work for anything with steps or a list: a process, a breakdown, a set of examples. (If you use Buffer, you can schedule PDF carousels to LinkedIn from the web app.) Video sits at number two. My colleague Kirsti Lang, who dug into these numbers in our format study , put it well: "carousels for engagement and video for reach is a solid combination." Text-only posts come last on engagement, but they're the fastest to make, which is always a win in my book. And link posts are near the bottom for the reasons in the previous tip. So it's worth knowing what you're getting from each format and trying a few. Post consistently (and more often than you might think) Posting more often doesn't hurt your reach on LinkedIn. When we looked at more than 2 million posts from over 94,000 LinkedIn accounts, posting two to five times a week got about 1,182 more impressions per post than posting once a week. Six to 10 posts a week got about 5,001 more, and 11 or more got nearly 17,000 more per post, plus about three times the engagements. That held whether an account had 500 followers or 50,000. That doesn't mean you need to post every day. We recommend two posts a week while you're building the habit, and more once you know you can keep the quality up. I personally post around four times a week. As for when to post, our analysis of 4.8 million posts found that weekdays between 3 p.m. and 8 p.m. (in your local time) did best, with Wednesday at 4 p.m. the strongest single slot. Mornings, the old default, did worse than they used to. You also don't need to sit by your phone for a "golden hour" after you post. LinkedIn has never confirmed one, though replying to early comments can definitely help keep a post going. 🎥 We made videos on both: how often to post on LinkedIn and the best time to post on LinkedIn . 📚 Or read the full studies on how often to post and the best time to post on LinkedIn. 12. Your analytics show you what your audience actually responds to, which beats any blog post's advice (this one included). Once a week or so, look at: Which posts got the most reactions, comments, and reposts, and what topic and format each one was. Which posts got the most impressions, and what their hooks were. Which posts got the most saves and sends. LinkedIn added both in September 2025 , and they're a good sign that a post was useful enough to keep or pass on. Who your posts reached. Since June 2026, LinkedIn has been rolling out a breakdown of how much of your reach came from inside your network versus outside it, which is the closest thing you'll get to "how many potential new followers saw this." It also shows the top job titles, industries, and locations of the people who saw each post, which is handy if you're trying to reach a certain kind of client. Which posts brought in new followers. Turn conversations into followers Comments are one of the two things LinkedIn's algorithm is trying to predict (the other being whether someone stays on your post), so the conversations around your content matter almost as much as the content itself. In my experience, this is also where a lot of growth actually happens: in your replies, in other people's comment sections, and in the people you team up with. 13. Reply to your comments This is the tip with the clearest data behind it, so I'll start there. When we looked at nearly 2 million posts across six platforms, replying to comments lifted engagement on LinkedIn by about 30% . And about 83% of LinkedIn profiles did better when they replied, the highest share of any platform in the study. So it helps almost everyone who does it. Every reply is another comment on the post, and comments are one of the things the feed is looking for. A post with a conversation under it also looks more alive than one with the same number of likes and no replies. So set aside time to respond to every comment and DM that comes in on your posts. It's the single highest-leverage 20 minutes you can spend after publishing. A few things that make the replies count for more: Reply with more than "Thanks!" Ask a question back, add a detail, or disagree if you do to keep the conversation going. Reply to the ones that clearly took effort first. If someone wrote three sentences, they've given you an opening to write three back. Don't automate it. In February 2026, LinkedIn started limiting automated comments , keeping them out of the default "Most relevant" view and restricting accounts that keep posting them. A scripted "Great post!" can now do more harm than no reply at all. 🗨️ Buffer's Community pulls your LinkedIn comments (and the ones from your other social media) into one place, so nothing slips through. 14. Comment where your future followers already are Most of the people who'll follow you next haven't seen your profile yet. They're in the comments of accounts they already follow, and the easiest way to get in front of them is to be in those comments too. This works in both directions. When you leave a comment on a bigger account's post, everyone reading that thread sees it, and a good one gets sorted toward the top under "Most relevant." At the same time, your own connections and followers may see that post in their feed with "[your name] commented on this" above it. So one thoughtful comment can put you in front of someone else's audience and remind your own that you exist, without you posting anything. Start by finding the people whose audience overlaps with the one you want, whether that's other creators in your niche, leaders in your industry, or the kind of clients you'd love to work with. Then comment on their posts thoughtfully and often. The "thoughtfully" part matters more now, since LinkedIn's crackdown on automated comments means generic ones sink. What tends to work: Add something: a related example, a number, a counterpoint. If your comment could be pasted under any post, it's not doing much. Ask a real question. It gives the author a reason to reply, which puts your name in the thread twice. Skip the pitch. Someone else's comment section isn't the place to mention your newsletter. And when there's someone you'd like a real two-way relationship with, send them a connection request with a personal note. A message that's clearly written for them goes a long way. 15. Collaborate with other creators Commenting gets you in front of someone else's audience for a moment. Collaborating gets you introduced to it. Partnering with another creator on something (a post, a live, a newsletter swap, a series) puts your name in front of their followers with their endorsement attached, which is a very different thing from showing up in their comments. It's also, honestly, more fun than posting alone, and that tends to show in the content. And in some very welcome news, LinkedIn now supports collaboration natively. In June 2026, LinkedIn added collaborative posts: you can invite another member or a company page to co-author a post, and everyone involved is listed at the top of it. The post shows up in both of your networks, and LinkedIn has been fairly open that member profiles carry more reach than pages, so this is also the cleanest way for a page to borrow some. A few things that make a collaboration worth the effort: Pick people your audience would want to hear from, not people who do your job. Two content creators collaborating reach the same people twice. A content creator and, say, a founder who hires content creators reach two different rooms. Make it something neither of you would have posted alone. A conversation, a comparison, a "here's how we each do it." If either of you could have written it solo, the collaboration isn't adding anything. Keep the thread going afterward. Reply to each other's comments, tag each other when it's relevant, and do it again in a few months. 📚 Learn more about how collab posts work on LinkedIn . 16.
