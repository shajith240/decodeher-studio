FAQ: Instagram followers and getting paid Here’s a secret about making money on Instagram: your follower count doesn’t matter nearly as much as you’d think. Some monetization options kick in at 500 followers. Others don’t have a follower minimum at all. Below, we’ll break down the main ways to make money on Instagram, plus how many followers you need for each one. Key takeaways There are several ways to make money on Instagram. You can earn through the Gifts, Subscriptions, Badges, affiliate sales, brand deals, and more. If you’re a smaller creator, you can still get paid. Brand deals and affiliate marketing have no follower minimum. Engagement is your secret weapon. Brands want proof that your audience is paying attention, and engagement is one way to show it. How many followers do you need on Instagram to get paid? You don’t need a specific number of followers to make money on Instagram . Even with a small following, you can still earn through brand deals and affiliate marketing. It’s a different story if you want to use Instagram’s monetization tools. For example, you need 500 followers to receive Gifts, and 10,000 followers to unlock Subscriptions and Badges. Bottom line: Having more followers opens more doors, but you can start earning long before you hit the big numbers. Source: eMarketer At this stage, there are a few ways to work with brands. For example, you could get paid to share a sponsored post with your followers, or create user-generated content (UGC) for a brand to share on its own channels. With UGC, your follower count matters a lot less because brands are paying for the content you create, not access to your audience. Earn affiliate commissions Affiliate marketing lets you earn a commission when someone buys a product through your unique link or code. On Instagram, that might look like sharing your favorite running shoes in a Reel, then adding your affiliate link to your bio . Every time a follower buys through that link, you get a cut of the sale. Pro tip 💡: Make sure to disclose any affiliate links. Instagram requires it , and it’s also just good practice. Sell your own products or services If you have something of your own to sell, Instagram can also help you find customers, no matter how many followers you have. A personal trainer, for example, might share workout routines on Instagram, then point followers to one-on-one coaching in their bio. The upside is that you’re not waiting for Instagram or a brand to pay you. Your content brings people in, and your product does the rest. What are Instagram’s native monetization tools, and what do they require? Instagram offers four main ways to pay creators directly: Gifts, Subscriptions, Badges, and Bonuses. Most require a professional account, a minimum age of 18, and a certain number of followers. Bonuses are the exception, since they’re invite-only. Gifts (500 followers) Gifts is a feature that lets viewers tip you on your Reels. They buy Stars in the Instagram app, then use them to send Gifts on the Reels they love. To turn on Gifts, you’ll need: At least 500 followers A professional account To be 18 or older To live in a country where Gifts are available (full list here ) There are a couple of fine-print rules, too. Collab posts can’t earn Gifts, and neither can accounts that mainly post content for kids. It’s also worth noting that Instagram takes its cut when someone buys Stars. So if a fan sends a $10 Gift, don’t expect the full amount. Source: Meta Subscriptions (10,000 followers) Subscriptions let your biggest fans pay a monthly fee for exclusive content. You set the price, and they get access to features like subscriber-only Stories, posts, and Lives. Subscribers also get a special badge next to their name, so they stand out in your comments and DMs. To qualify, you’ll need: At least 10,000 followers A professional account To be 18 or older To live in an eligible country (full list here ) Note: Subscriptions aren’t available to accounts that mainly post content for kids. Badges (10,000 followers) While you’re hosting an Instagram Live, viewers can buy Badges for $0.99, $1.99, or $4.99. In return, they get a heart icon next to their username in the comments. And, if they ask a question during the Live, it gets highlighted. To qualify, you’ll need: At least 10,000 followers A professional account To be 18 or older Fans can buy as many Badges as they want, up to $250 per Live. As always, this feature isn’t available to accounts that mainly post content for kids. Bonus programs (invite-only) For a limited time, Instagram is testing bonuses that pay you for your content. There are two types: bonuses for Reels, and bonuses for carousels and single-image posts. But, there’s a catch. Bonuses are invite-only, so Instagram has to come to you. If you receive an invite, you’ll find it in your professional dashboard. Here’s how to qualify for an invite: Reels bonuses: At least 5 million views over the previous three consecutive months. This one is only available in the U.S. and South Korea. Photo and carousel bonuses: A minimum number of views per month for three consecutive months. In the U.S., for example, you need at least 5 million views per month . You’ll also need to be 18 or older and have a professional account. Why do brand deals and affiliate marketing have no follower minimum? Brand deals and affiliate marketing have no follower minimum because Instagram doesn’t run them. They’re agreements between you and a brand, so the brand decides who to work with, not Instagram. Brands care about fit, not just reach Follower count gets a lot of attention, but it rarely seals the deal. Brands want the right audience, not just a big one. That’s why they also look at factors like audience fit, engagement, and content quality. Brands only pay for results With affiliate marketing, you earn a commission when someone buys through your link or code. If nobody buys, the brand doesn’t pay. Since there’s almost no risk for the brand, there’s little reason to set a minimum. If your audience trusts your recommendations, that matters a lot more than how big it is. To start monetizing Instagram, start by switching to a professional account and picking a clear niche. Then, turn on any native features you qualify for, build a media kit, and start pitching brands directly. Here’s how it breaks down: Switch to a professional account Pick a niche Check your monetization eligibility Build a media kit that leads with engagement Pitch brand deals and affiliate partnerships 1. Switch to a Professional (Creator or Business) account Personal accounts can’t access native monetization tools on Instagram, so you’ll need to switch to a Creator or Business account. The switch only takes a minute in your account settings. And as a bonus, you’ll get access to insights, which come in handy later. 2. Pick a niche Your niche is the main topic your content revolves around. Think skincare, budget travel, home workouts, or even something as specific as sourdough baking. It might feel limiting at first, but a clear niche makes it easier to attract an audience. Followers know what to expect from you, so they’re more likely to stick around. 3. Check your monetization eligibility Open your professional dashboard to see which monetization tools are available to you. If you have 500 followers, start with Gifts. At 10,000 followers, you can add Subscriptions and Badges. Each tool needs to be turned on separately, and you’ll need to set up a payout account before any money can reach you. Don’t worry if your options look a little thin at first. Your list will grow as your audience does. 4. Build a media kit that leads with engagement, not just followers A media kit is basically your résumé as a creator. It tells people who you are and why they should work with you. Most media kits include: A short bio Audience demographics Your engagement rate and average views A few examples of your best content Any past brand partnerships or results Put your engagement numbers front and center, especially if your follower count is on the smaller side. A strong engagement rate can make a brand look twice, even if your audience is still growing. 5. Pitch brand deals and affiliate partnerships directly You don’t have to wait for brands to find you. Reach out to the ones you already use and love, since those partnerships feel the most natural. As for affiliate partnerships, look for programs from brands in your niche. Many companies run their own, like Amazon’s affiliate program. What do brands look at besides follower count when vetting creators? Brands look at follower count to get a quick sense of a creator’s reach, but they rarely stop there. Engagement, audience fit, and how the creator handles brand work can matter just as much. We spoke with Sarah Moore , Senior Director of Digital Strategy at G7 Marketing , about what brands look at beyond the headline number. They balance follower count with engagement Follower count is a quick way to gauge a creator’s potential reach. But as Moore points out, it only tells you so much. “Somebody’s follower count is simply a metric that tells you how big the room is,” she shares. “Engagement rate tells you how many of them are actually listening.” For Moore, this is where a smaller creator can nudge out a much bigger one: “I’d rather partner with a creator who has 2,000 real fans than one with 500,000 followers whose content is no longer showing on their audience’s feed.” They look at the comment section There’s a big difference between following a creator and actually connecting with them. The comment section is where brands can spot that difference. Moore recommends looking at the conversations happening there. Are people asking questions, replying to the creator, or talking to each other? That kind of back-and-forth gives you a much better sense of how connected an audience really is. They look at how the creator handles brand work Brands also want to see how a creator’s audience reacts to sponsored posts. Ultimately, the best partnerships feel like a natural part of the creator’s content, not an interruption. “If their posts feel like a scripted break from their real content, their audience will notice,” Moore observes.
