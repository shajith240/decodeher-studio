# Reel storytelling craft: making scripts sound like a guy talking

Research for @thedecodeher. Written 2026-10-14. Problem: the owner says our scripts sound AI-written and "bookish", like an essay read out loud. This file explains why, gives the ban list, and gives templates to write from.

**How sure we are.** Each claim is marked one of three ways:
- **[source]**: a primary source or a well-documented secondary one, with the URL.
- **[practitioner]**: creator or marketing advice. Common, but nobody has tested it properly.
- **[ours]**: our own reasoning or drafts.

Hook "retention %" figures from vendor blogs are left out on purpose, because none of them shows its data.

---

## 0. The diagnosis in one paragraph

Our lines read like writing because they use the grammar of writing. Douglas Biber's corpus work on speech and writing found one main dimension that separates them. "Involved" talk, like face-to-face conversation, is full of contractions, "I" and "you", present tense, questions, dropped "that", and verbs like *think*, *know* and *feel*. "Informational" prose, like academic writing, is dense with nouns, long words and adjectives [source: Biber, *Variation across Speech and Writing*, 1988; feature list summarised at https://www.comp.nus.edu.sg/~kanmy/dossier/courses/5244_2006/lectureNotes/w9-plagiarism_files/slide0011.htm].

Look at "Closeness comes from questions that go one level deeper." The subject is an abstract noun, there are no people in it, there's no time or place, and it's built like a proverb. That's informational prose, so it sounds like an essay because grammatically it is one.

Matthew Dicks' "Dinner Test" is the fix: a performed story should sound the way you'd tell it to a friend over dinner [source: Shortform summary of *Storyworthy*, https://www.shortform.com/blog/storytelling-steps/]. Nobody at dinner says "closeness comes from questions." They say "So I asked her why nursing, and she talked for like twenty minutes."

**The one-line rule:** every line has to be something a real guy could say out loud to his friend, about a specific girl, on a specific night.

## 1. How spoken short-form storytelling works

### 1.1 Spoken vs written English: what to do

| Feature of real speech | What it sounds like | Why it works | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Contractions, always | "she's", "you're", "didn't", "it's gonna" | Uncontracted speech sounds read or angry | Biber D1 [source]; radio-writing guides say to use contractions freely: https://edis.ifas.ufl.edu/publication/wc193 |
| Fragments | "Three weeks. Zero dates." "Crazy." | People don't speak in full clauses. A fragment acts as emphasis | Ear-writing guides: short, self-contained chunks, mixed sentence lengths. https://tools.cdc.gov/medialibrary/docs/AudioScriptWritingGuide.pdf [source] |
| Run-ons joined with "and" / "so" | "and she goes... and I'm like... so I just leave it" | This is how spoken narrative chains its events | Labov's "complicating action" is a run of clauses told in order [source: summaries of Labov & Waletzky 1967, e.g. https://cle.ens-lyon.fr/anglais/fichiers/william-labov-a-structural-analysis-of-narrative-mode-de-compatibilite-769-_1366115192183-pdf] |
| Switching to the present tense | "So yesterday she texts me..." "And she goes..." | The conversational historical present. Wolfson found that the *switch* from past to present marks the important events of a told story | Wolfson 1979/1982, summarised in https://freidok.uni-freiburg.de/dnb/download/5343 [source]; Dicks recommends present tense for immediacy, https://www.shortform.com/blog/storytelling-steps/ [source] |
| Direct speech | 'She goes, "ahh this week's crazy for me."' | Wolfson lists quoted speech, asides, repetition and sound effects as the "performance" devices of oral stories | Same Wolfson sources [source] |
| Specifics: names, times, places, exact texts | "1:14 a.m.", "a cat falling off a couch", "Thursday or Saturday?" | Specifics make a story feel true and make the viewer think "that's literally me". Narrative transportation goes up with believable detail | Green & Brock / Van Laer, already in `relatable-storytelling.md` [source]; a vague "I've been through a lot" storytime underperforms specific stakes [practitioner: https://www.nemovideo.com/th/templates/storytime-tiktok-template] |
| Self-interruption, self-correction | "Two weeks. No, three." "And she, okay, she didn't even say no." | Shows the speaker thinking in real time. Nobody self-corrects in an essay | [ours], from the Biber/Wolfson features above |
| Asides | "(and yeah, I checked when she was last online)" | An aside is part of the performance and lets the speaker judge his own story | Wolfson [source] |
| Rhetorical questions | "What's she supposed to wonder about now?" | WH-questions are a marker of involved speech | Biber D1 [source] |
| Direct address | "you", "bro", "man", "look" | Second person and discourse markers are involved-speech features | Biber D1 [source] |
| Verbs over nouns | "she stopped asking you stuff" instead of "her interest declined" | Nouns are what informational prose is made of | Biber D1 [source] |

**Using "bro", "like", "man".** They're markers, so treat them like salt. One or two per 35 s reel is enough, and none in the first second of the hook. They also have to fit the narrator: a deep, calm voice saying "bro" four times sounds like a parody [ours].

### 1.2 Story mechanics from the experts

- **Dicks, *Storyworthy*:** every story turns on a "five-second moment" of change or realisation, and the opening should be the opposite of that moment [source: https://www.supersummary.com/storyworthy/summary/]. Start as close to the end as you can, with forward motion [source: https://calv.info/bookshelf/storyworthy]. He also uses the "Elephant": tell the audience early what they're waiting for. Don't announce "this is a story", and stay with the Dinner Test (above).
  - For a 35 s reel, our five-second moment is the realisation: she has time for memes but not for you. Open on the opposite: "She's into me."
- **Trey Parker & Matt Stone (NYU class):** if the words between your beats are "and then", the story is broken. They should be "but" or "therefore" [source: https://thehustle.co/the-storytelling-secrets-that-netted-matt-stone-and-trey-parker-600-million-dollars; https://writerunboxed.com/?p=35610].
  - Note the nuance. "And then" is fine as *spoken texture* inside a beat ("and then she sends another one"). It's banned as the *logic* between beats.
- **Kallaway (short form, 1B+ views): the hook is a curiosity loop in three moves** [source: summaries of his hooks video, https://opentools.ai/youtube-summary/how-to-create-irresistible-hooks-and-blow-up-your-content; podcast at https://www.castmagic.io/podcast/rhythm-hooks-and-a-billion-views-kane-kallaways-short-form-video-content-secrets]:
  1. **Context lean-in:** make the topic obvious and find common ground.
  2. **Scroll-stop interjection:** one line, usually a contrast word ("but").
  3. **Contrarian snapback:** go the opposite way from the lean-in, but stay on topic.
  - He also says: put **3-5 words on screen**, short choppy hook sentences that get longer later, and the first value within about 4 seconds ("compress speed to value"). On-screen text, motion and voice should all fire together.
  - His worked example: "The tech in the Vegas Sphere is insane, biggest screen ever built... but get this, the screen is actually the least impressive part."
- **Hormozi:** cut the lead-in and start on the hook. One cited example is a video that hit 780K views after the intro was removed [practitioner: https://www.skool.com/lead-gen-mastery-4994/why-your-content-fails-in-the-first-3-seconds-and-how-to-fix-it]. He uses Proof-Promise-Plan for persuasion [source summary: https://ampifire.com/blog/alex-hormozi-proof-promise-plan-framework-to-sell-your-idea/]. A "70 hooks" list couldn't be verified.
- **Brendan Kane, *Hook Point*:** people decide in about 3 seconds. A hook point can be a sentence, a number, a visual or a character, and testing beats instinct [source summaries: https://www.shortform.com/summary/hook-point-summary-brendan-kane, https://summaries.com/blog/hook-point].
- **Ali Abdaal (HIVES = Hook, Intro, Value, End screen):** the hook makes a clear promise [source summary: https://summify.io/discover/how-to-easily-script-youtube-videos-to-get-more-views-2rzzRxixzq4/]. It's long-form, so only the "clear promise" part carries over.
- **Open loops (Jay Acunzo, often confused with Jay Clouse):** the payoff has to match how strongly the loop was opened. A big loop with a small payoff feels like clickbait [source: https://jayacunzo.com/blog/open-loops-a-simple-technique-to-make-your-stories-more-gripping]. No Jay Clouse or Tim Denning source on this was found, so nothing is attributed to them.
- **Paddy Galloway:** a "3.3B Shorts, 70-90% viewed-vs-swiped" figure goes around, but only second-hand [unverified: https://virvid.ai/blog/first-3-seconds-hook-faceless-shorts-2026]. Don't quote it.
- **What Instagram ranks on:** Mosseri (Jan 2025) named watch time, likes per reach and sends per reach as the top Reels signals [source, already logged in `relatable-storytelling.md`]. A story that makes him send it to his boy ("this is literally you") beats a clever aphorism.

### 1.3 Viral storytime, case study: Reesa Teesa, "Who TF Did I Marry?!"

The series has about 200M views across roughly 50 parts and 8 hours [source: https://www.fox9.com/news/who-tf-did-i-marry-the-viral-52-part-tiktok-series-explained]. What we can take from it:
- **The title is a spoken confession, said by the person it happened to.** It isn't a thesis. Nobody would title it "Deception in Modern Marriage."
- It started as a reply to a trend ("worst thing an ex did"), so the hook was a question the audience was already asking.
- The story moves through **small concrete lies that build up**, then a reveal (a stolen identity). Each part ends on an open loop, which is what makes people binge it.
- She's on camera, front-facing, low production, talking the way she'd talk to a friend. A July 2026 roundup says single-take, low-polish content beats polished edits on Reels [practitioner: https://ugccopilot.ai/blog/viral-hooks-that-convert/].
- **For us:** our visuals are polished, so the *words* have to bring the rawness.

### 1.4 Pacing and where the open loops go (35 s reel)

The budget is **150 wpm = 2.5 words/second**. A slow, deep AI voice with pauses lands nearer 135-145 wpm, so **aim for 80-88 words for 35 s.**

| Time | Job | Open loop |
|---|---|---|
| 0-2 s | Hook: a scene already in motion, plus 3-5 words on screen | **Loop 1 opens:** "what's going on with her?" |
| 2-10 s | Scene: two concrete details and one exact quote | Loop 1 deepens |
| ~10-12 s | **BUT** turn: the contradiction | **Loop 2 opens:** "so what does it mean?" This is the mid-video re-hook. Change the visual here |
| 12-24 s | The read: what's actually going on, in his words, with one personal aside | Loop 2 pays off at about 20-24 s |
| 24-31 s | The move: one thing he can do, with an exact line to send | Small loop: "and if she dodges that..." |
| 31-35 s | Payoff + CTA ("follow for more", per our CTA rule) | Close every loop. Ideally the last words feed back into the first |

Re-hooking every 5-8 s and the 3-second drop-off cliff are both **[practitioner]**. Use them, but check them against our own retention graphs.

## 2. The tells of AI-written scripts: ban list with human alternatives

**Background.** Wikipedia's editor guide "Signs of AI writing" lists, among others:
- "AI vocabulary" (*delve, crucial, pivotal, tapestry, testament, showcase, underscore, intricate*)
- dodging plain "is" ("serves as", "stands as")
- negative parallelism ("Not X, but Y")
- the rule of three
- shallow "-ing" analysis tails ("highlighting...")
- promotional adjectives
- em-dash overuse (now listed as a historical tell)

Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing. The guide warns that no single sign proves anything. It's the pile-up that gives text away, and our scripts pile them up.

| # | Pattern (BAN) | Bad example (our style) | Why it reads AI | Human alternative |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Tidy triplets / parallelism** | "She laughs at your jokes. She replies in minutes. She sends you memes." | Rule of three plus identical sentence frames. Real speech is uneven | Two items, or one item and a longer one: "She replies in like two minutes. And she sends you memes. At one a.m." |
| 2 | **"It's not X, it's Y" / "Not X. Y."** | "It's not attention. It's boredom." | Negative parallelism is the most recognisable LLM move | State the Y with a concrete detail and drop the X: "Bro, you're her one a.m. entertainment." |
| 3 | **Aphorism endings** | "Busy is an answer." "Actions speak louder." | A proverb sums things up from outside the story. Also a recycled line: Behrendt (2004) wrote "'Busy' is another word for 'asshole'" in *He's Just Not That Into You*, https://www.goodreads.com/quotes/131362 | End on a scene, a line to send, or the callback: "Thursday or Saturday. She dodges that too? You're done." |
| 4 | **Abstract nouns as subjects** (*closeness, attraction, connection, interest, intimacy, effort, validation, energy*) | "Closeness comes from questions that go one level deeper." | Nominalisation is the core of informational prose (Biber) | Put a person in subject position doing a verb: "Ask her why she picked nursing. Watch her light up." |
| 5 | **"Here's the thing" family** ("Here's the truth / the kicker / the reality", "The truth is", "Let's be honest", "Here's why", "Let that sink in") | "Here's the thing: attention is cheap." | Signposts that delay the content. Now read as AI and as bad hooks | Just say it. Or use a real-speech marker that doesn't promise a reveal: "Look." "Bro." "Okay so." |
| 6 | **Perfect grammar, same sentence length** | Eight sentences, all 7-10 words, all complete | Monotone rhythm. Radio guides warn against same-length sentences: https://campus.recit.qc.ca/pluginfile.php/111717/mod_page/content/12/writing_ear.pdf | Mix one-word lines ("Crazy."), a run-on with "and... and...", fragments, and one question |
| 7 | **No specifics** ("she", "a girl", "a text", "later") | "She replies late at night." | Generic writing can't be pictured or felt as "that's me" | Time, object, exact words: "1:14 a.m. A cat falling off a couch." |
| 8 | **Moralising / lecture voice** ("You deserve...", "Respect yourself", "Real men...", "Know your worth") | "When someone wants to see you, they make room." | A narrator explaining life from a podium | Speak as a peer who's been there: "I've been that guy. Three weeks of memes. Zero dates." |
| 9 | **Em-dash rhythm** | "She's always busy — but never too busy to text." | A historical AI tell (Wikipedia). TTS treats dashes inconsistently (ElevenLabs docs) | Full stop, ellipsis, or a new line: "She's always busy. Except at one a.m., apparently." |
| 10 | **AI words** (*truly, genuinely, navigate, delve, crucial, pivotal, profound, intentional, authentic, resonate, journey, landscape, foster, dynamic, nuanced, unlock, elevate, embrace, ultimately, essentially, in today's world*) | "Truly secure men navigate this differently." | Wikipedia's "AI vocabulary" list. No guy talks like this | Use the plain word or nothing: "really", "actually", "deal with", or cut it |
| 11 | **Metaphor of the week** ("That's her opening the door", "the door is closing", "the spark") | "That's her opening the door." | A stock metaphor stands in for describing the actual moment | Describe the actual moment: "She just told you she's free Saturday. Without you asking." |
| 12 | **"When X, Y" general rules** | "When she knows exactly how you feel, there's nothing left to wonder about." | A conditional law of nature with no scene | Make it one guy's mistake: "You told her you like her on day three? What's she supposed to wonder about now?" |
| 13 | **Vague "she/they/people" weasels** ("Studies show", "Most women", "Psychologists say") | "Psychologists call this intermittent reinforcement." | An authority with no name | Name it once, plainly, only if it's real and adds something: "There's a name for this. Intermittent reinforcement. Same thing that keeps you pulling a slot machine." |
| 14 | **Narrator announcing the lesson** ("The lesson?", "The takeaway:", "Remember this:") | "The lesson? Watch the calendar." | An essay conclusion | Give the action as an instruction to a friend: "Ask once. Give her two days. Then leave it." |

**Fast detector [ours].** Read the script out loud into your phone and send it as a voice note to a friend. Mark any line you'd be embarrassed to say in that voice note.

## 3. Hooks that work now (2025-2026)

**Ground rules first.**
- Reels gives you roughly 1 s to land the line and about 3 s to promise the payoff [practitioner: https://ugccopilot.ai/hooks/instagram-reels/].
- Keep spoken hooks to **7-12 words** [practitioner: https://www.teleprompter.com/blog/hooks-for-instagram-reels].
- On-screen text should be 3-5 words (Kallaway) and has to match the spoken first line. A mismatch is listed as a cause of drop-off (teleprompter.com).

Marking used below:
- **[real]** = a documented title or line from a real viral video or trend, with source.
- **[draft]** = written by us for this page. Search can't reliably verify exact viral Reel captions, and we won't invent attributions.

### 3.1 Hook categories

**A. In-medias-res scene (start mid-action, second person).** *Best fit for us.*
- [real, Kallaway pattern] "The tech in the Vegas Sphere is insane... but get this..." (context, then snap)
- [draft] "It's one a.m. and she's sending you a cat falling off a couch."
- [draft] "You're on the third date and she just asked for the check."
- [draft] "She's been on read for six hours and you're rewriting the same text."

**B. Confession / "I did the dumb thing".**
- [real] "Who TF Did I Marry?!" (Reesa Teesa, ~200M views, https://www.fox9.com/news/who-tf-did-i-marry-the-viral-52-part-tiktok-series-explained)
- [real, trend format] "I was today years old when..." (template documented at https://ugccopilot.ai/hooks/instagram-reels/)
- [draft] "I let a girl string me along for three months on memes alone."
- [draft] "I double-texted a girl on her birthday. Here's what she sent back."

**C. Contrarian / snapback.**
- [real] Steve Cole's viral TikTok: he wants to date someone "too busy to date me" (https://www.bustle.com/wellness/too-busy-to-date-expert-tiktok)
- [real, line] Behrendt: "'Busy' is another word for 'asshole'" (book, 2004; still recycled in reels)
- [draft] "Texting her every day is the reason she's losing interest."
- [draft] "The girl who texts you first isn't always the one who likes you."

**D. Call-out ("if she does this...", "if you're the guy who...").** Strong for sends.
- [real, template] "If you struggle with ___, this is probably the missing piece." (https://www.teleprompter.com/blog/hooks-for-instagram-reels)
- [draft] "If she only texts you after eleven, watch this."
- [draft] "If she says 'haha' and nothing else, read this before you reply."
- [draft] "Send this to the friend who's still waiting on 'busy girl.'"

**E. POV.** Works only if the scene is specific. A lazy POV gets skipped [practitioner: ugccopilot].
- [real, format] "POV: you walk into an interview and your mind goes blank." (teleprompter.com example)
- [draft] "POV: it's 1 a.m. and she's laughing at your memes but 'busy' all week."
- [draft] "POV: you finally ask her out and she replies with a meme."

**F. Named rule ("the X rule").** Use only if the rule is concrete and checkable.
- [real, template] "Use this one rule to write emails people actually reply to." (teleprompter.com)
- [draft] "The two-day rule: if she won't name a day, she's not coming."
- [draft] "The 'Thursday or Saturday' rule. Steal it."

**G. Specific number / result.** Real-feeling numbers beat round ones [practitioner: https://www.opus.pro/blog/tiktok-hooks-hijacking-algorithm-2026].
- [real] MrBeast titles: "I Spent 50 Hours Buried Alive", "$1 vs $1,000,000 Hotel Room!", "Ages 1 - 100 Fight For $500,000"
- [draft] "Three weeks. Forty-one memes. Zero dates."
- [draft] "She took four hours to reply and seven seconds to say no."

**H. Tested-it / experiment.**
- [real, template] "I did ___ for 30 days, results surprised me." (teleprompter.com)
- [draft] "I stopped replying to her one a.m. memes for a week. Day four, she called."
- [draft] "I asked her out with two days to pick from instead of 'sometime.' Here's what changed."

**I. Identity / "tell me without telling me" / "this is your sign".**
- [real] #TellMeWithoutTellingMe, about 1.8B views by Jan 2021 (https://thehowler.org/14030/uncategorized/tellmewithouttellingme/)
- [real, template] "This is your sign to stop ___." (ugccopilot)
- [draft] "This is your sign to stop answering her one a.m. memes."

**J. "You're losing her because..." (diagnosis).**
- [real, template] "This is why your resume gets ignored in the first 10 seconds." / "Here is why your ___ is not working" (teleprompter.com; ugccopilot)
- [draft] "You're losing her because you answer in eleven seconds."
- [draft] "She went cold the day you said 'whatever you want.'"

**K. The "3 seconds" pattern.**
- [draft] "Give me three seconds and you'll know if she's actually busy."
- [draft] "In three seconds, she decided you were the meme guy."
- Use sparingly. It's a meta-hook, so it ages fast [ours].

### 3.2 Which suit a dating-psychology storyteller

Order of fit for a faceless, deep-voice, silhouette page [ours]:
1. **In-medias-res scene (A).**
2. **Confession (B).**
3. **Call-out (D).**
4. **Diagnosis (J).**
5. **Contrarian (C).**
6. **Specific number (G).**

Rank them this way because they open a *story*, our visuals can show them (a silhouette, a phone glow, a timestamp), and they get sends ("this is you").

- **Use with care:** POV (needs real specifics) and named rule (needs a real, checkable rule).
- **Avoid:** experiment hooks (H) unless the narrator persona actually "tested" something. A faceless AI voice claiming experiments sounds fake.

### 3.3 First 1-2 seconds: three hooks that have to agree

| Layer | Job | Example for the meme story |
|---|---|---|
| **Verbal** (first spoken line) | Puts us inside a scene with a time and an object | "It's one fourteen a.m. She sends you a cat falling off a couch." |
| **Visual** (frame 1, already moving) | The same scene: a glowing phone in a dark room, timestamp 1:14, silhouette on the bed | Phone lights up the silhouette's face, meme bubble pops in |
| **Text** (3-5 words, big) | Names the tension, not the topic | "MEMES AT 1AM. 'BUSY' FRIDAY." |

Rule: all three point at the same thing. If the voice says "1 a.m." and the screen says "Signs she's not interested", that's a mismatch and people swipe (teleprompter.com).

### 3.4 Burnt-out hooks: don't open with these

None of these has been tested head to head. They're practitioner consensus plus Kallaway's "compress speed to value" logic: anything that delays the content costs viewers.
- "Did you know...?" A quiz-show opener with no scene. Kallaway's logic says it wastes the first second.
- "Here's why..." / "Here's the thing" / "Let me tell you..." / "Let's talk about..." These announce content instead of giving it.
- "Hi guys, today we're going to..." Called out as dead by 2026 roundups (https://www.sotrender.com/blog/?p=56913).
- "Stop scrolling!" / "Wait for it" / "Watch till the end" / "You won't believe..." These are commands, not reasons to stay.
- "Nobody talks about..." / "Why is nobody talking about..." Flagged as degrading quickly with repeated use on the same audience (ugccopilot). Fine once a month, not weekly.
- "Ladies and gentlemen" / "Men, listen up" / "Fellas," on their own. Fine only if a scene follows in the same breath.
- A lazy "POV: you're a man in 2026". No scene, so people skip it.

## 4. Story structures for 30-45 s reels

**The structures:**
- **In medias res:** start inside the complicating action (Labov) and leave out the orientation. Time and place go *inside* the action line ("it's one a.m. and...").
- **But / therefore chain:** each beat joins to the next with "but" or "so" (Parker/Stone).
- **Open loop with payoff:** promise something specific at 0-3 s and pay it off at 70-80% of the runtime (Acunzo: the payoff has to match the size of the loop).
- **Twist ending:** set up an assumption (Dicks: the beginning is the opposite of the end) and flip it in the final 5 s.
- **Callback to hook:** the last line repeats or answers an exact phrase from the first line.
- **Loop ending:** the last sentence runs grammatically into the first one, so a replay sounds continuous. That's a watch-time play [practitioner].

At 150 wpm, 1 s ≈ 2.5 words. For a deep TTS voice with pauses, budget about 2.3 words/s.

### Template 1: "Cold open, BUT, read, move" (35 s, about 85 words)

| Time | Beat | Words | Rules |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0-2 s | **Cold open:** time + object + action, second person | 6-10 | No setup. Specific time or quote |
| 2-9 s | **Scene:** two more details, one exact text in quotes | 15-18 | Present tense. "Then..." allowed inside the beat |
| 9-14 s | **BUT:** the contradiction, said as dialogue | 10-12 | Visual change here (re-hook) |
| 14-22 s | **Read:** what it means, as a peer. One aside or confession | 18-20 | No abstract nouns. One "look," / "bro," max |
| 22-31 s | **Move:** one action plus the exact line to send | 18-20 | Concrete words he can copy |
| 31-35 s | **Close + CTA:** callback to the hook + "follow for more" | 8-10 | Spoken CTA per our rule |

### Template 2: "Open loop, story, payoff" (40 s, about 95 words)

| Time | Beat | Words | Rules |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0-3 s | **Loop:** "There's one text that tells you if she's actually busy." | 8-12 | The promise must be specific and deliverable |
| 3-12 s | **Story start, in medias res:** a guy (or "you") mid-situation | 20-22 | Name one detail per sentence |
| 12-20 s | **Complication (BUT):** the thing that breaks his read | 18-20 | Quote her exact words |
| 20-30 s | **Payoff:** the text, said word for word, and why it works, in one line | 22-25 | Pay off the loop here, at about 75% |
| 30-37 s | **What happens next (SO):** two outcomes, one line each | 15 | No moral |
| 37-40 s | **CTA** | 6-8 | "Follow for more. Part two's the exit text." |

### Template 3: "Assumption, twist, loop" (30 s, about 70 words)

| Time | Beat | Words | Rules |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0-2 s | **Assumption line:** what he believes ("She's into me.") | 4-8 | Dicks: open on the opposite of the ending |
| 2-12 s | **Evidence:** 2-3 quick concrete proofs he'd cite | 22-25 | Uneven list (not a tidy triplet) |
| 12-20 s | **Twist:** one fact that flips it | 18-20 | Hard visual change. Short sentence |
| 20-27 s | **Re-read:** the same evidence seen again, in one line | 14-16 | Callback to the exact proof words |
| 27-30 s | **Loop line + CTA:** the last words lead into the first line | 6-8 | e.g. "...and you'll still think..." then the hook "She's into me." starts again. Spoken CTA goes before the loop line, or as a caption plus a one-word spoken "Follow." Test both |

## 5. Writing for an AI narrator so it sounds human

Our voice is Fish Audio. s1 is deprecated (it retires 31 Dec 2026), and s2.1-pro uses `[bracket]` natural-language cues (see `voices.md`). The rules below come from the Fish and ElevenLabs docs plus our own testing notes.

### 5.1 Breath and pauses

- **One idea per line.** Write the script as a list of lines, not paragraphs. Each full stop is a breath. Long sentences make TTS rush and flatten [source: ear-writing guides above; Fish recommends proper punctuation for natural pauses and splitting long text, https://docs.fish.audio/resources/best-practices/text-to-speech].
- **Ellipsis (...)** gives hesitation or weight before a reveal. It's less consistent than tags, so regenerate if it misfires [source: https://elevenlabs.io/docs/overview/capabilities/text-to-speech/best-practices]. Use **at most 2 per reel**.
- **No em dashes.** Dashes are inconsistent in TTS and an AI tell on screen. Use a full stop or an ellipsis.
- **Fish pause cues:** `[short pause]` / `[long pause]`-style cues exist [source: Fish emotion reference, https://docs.fish.audio/resources/emotion-reference]. Use one, before the turn, and only if punctuation isn't enough.
- **Chunk size about 200 characters** (Fish default `chunk_length` 200). Keep each generated chunk to 1-3 lines so the prosody stays fresh.

### 5.2 Emphasis

- **End-focus [ours, based on English intonation].** English puts the main stress on the last content word of a clause, so put the word you want hit **at the end of a short sentence**, or give it a line of its own: "Crazy." "Zero dates." "Thursday or Saturday."
- **No ALL CAPS for Fish.** Fish docs say caps read as shouting [source: Fish best practices, summarised above]. ElevenLabs says caps add emphasis. The engines disagree, and since we're on Fish, skip caps. (On-screen text can be caps. The TTS input can't.)
- **Delivery cues go at the start of a sentence**, one per sentence, and rarely: "Less is more", "space out emotional changes" [source: https://docs.fish.audio/developer-guide/best-practices/emotion-control]. For our narrator, two cues per reel at most: e.g. `[dry, amused]` on the "Crazy." line and `[low, matter-of-fact]` on the move.
- **Questions rise.** Use a real question mark when you want the voice to lift ("Drinks Friday?"). Don't use one when you want flat sarcasm. Write that as a statement.

### 5.3 Words TTS gets wrong: write them out

| Write in TTS input | Not | Why |
|---|---|---|
| "one fourteen a.m." / "one in the morning" | "1:14am", "1am" | Number and abbreviation normalisation varies [source: ElevenLabs normalisation; Fish `normalize` option] |
| "L-M-A-O" or just paraphrase ("she sends 'this is so you' with like six crying faces") | "LMAOOO", "lol", "ngl", "fr", "tbh", "istg" | Gets spelled out or garbled. Put the real slang **on screen**, keep the voice clean |
| "twenty bucks" | "$20" | Normalisation |
| "versus" | "vs" | |
| "D-M", "Insta" | "DM'd", "IG" | Test once and save to a pronunciation list |
| Avoid or rephrase homographs: *read* (past vs present), *close* (near vs shut; this is in our niche!), *live*, *lead*, *tear*, *wind*, *content*, *present*, *object* | | The model guesses from context and guesses wrong. "She's close" vs "close the deal" |
| Names: pick easy, unambiguous ones (Sara, Maya, Jess) | Unusual spellings | Fish/ElevenLabs suggest respelling or phoneme tags for hard words; easier to avoid them |
| Emoji, hashtags, brackets that aren't cues | | Read aloud, or break the cue syntax |

Generate 2-3 takes of each line that matters and pick the best one. Both vendors recommend this.

### 5.4 Small imperfections: on purpose, but rationed

**Allowed list:** "look," / "honestly," / "bro," / "man," / "okay so" / "like," / "and yeah..." / "wait," / one self-correction ("Two weeks. No... three.")

**Ration [ours]:**
- At most **2 markers per 35 s** and **1 self-correction or aside**.
- **None in the first 2 seconds.** The hook has to be clean and fast.
- Never stack them ("look, honestly, bro,").
- **Never use "honestly" before a claim that isn't a confession.** It makes everything else sound less honest.

Where they belong: just before the confession ("Look, I've been that guy"), or after the twist as a reaction ("...and yeah. That's the whole thing.").

### 5.5 Script QA checklist (run before voicing)

1. Read aloud at speed. Does any line sound like it's from a book? Rewrite it.
2. Is there at least **one time, one object, and one exact quote**?
3. Do the beats join with **but / so**, not "and then"?
4. Ctrl-F for these and fix every hit:
   - " — "
   - "It's not" / "not just"
   - "Here's"
   - "truly", "genuinely", "navigate", "connection", "closeness", "attraction", "energy", "deserve", "the truth is"
5. Count the triplets. Max one, and it must be uneven.
6. Sentence lengths: at least one 1-2 word line and one 12+ word run-on.
7. Does the last line answer or echo the first line?
8. Word count 80-88 for 35 s.
9. Spoken CTA with "follow for more" is present (CTA rule).
10. Would he send it to his friend with "this is literally you"? If not, the scene isn't specific enough.

## 6. Before / after

### 6.1 The four hated lines

**1. "Closeness comes from questions that go one level deeper."**
Problem: abstract noun subject, no people, a proverb.
- **A:** "She says she's a nurse. Most guys go 'oh nice.' You go, 'okay... why nursing?' Watch her face change."
- **B:** "Don't ask what she does, bro. Ask why she picked it. That's when she talks for twenty minutes straight."

**2. "That's her opening the door."**
Problem: stock metaphor, nothing actually happens.
- **A:** "Bro. She just told you she's free Saturday. You didn't even ask. Ask her out."
- **B:** "She brought up that taco place. Twice. Man, she's literally handing you the date."

**3. "When she knows exactly how you feel, there's nothing left to wonder about."**
Problem: a conditional law of nature, no scene.
- **A:** "You told her 'I really like you' on day three? Yeah... what's she supposed to wonder about now?"
- **B:** "Look, if you text her good morning, good night, and 'miss you' before date two... she's got nothing left to figure out. You did it for her."

**4. "Busy is an answer."**
Problem: an aphorism, and a recycle of Behrendt's 2004 line.
- **A:** "She's been 'busy' three weekends in a row. Bro. That *is* the answer."
- **B:** "Nobody's busy for a month straight. She'd find twenty minutes for a guy she actually wanted to see."

### 6.2 Full 35 s script: "she texts you memes at 1 a.m. but says she's busy when you ask her out"

**Old bookish style** (what we've been writing; deliberately reconstructed to show the tells):

> She sends you memes at one in the morning. She laughs at your jokes. She replies within minutes. But when you ask her out, she's busy. Here's the thing: attention isn't the same as interest. Late-night texting is low-effort connection. It costs her nothing. A date costs her time, and time is where real interest shows. When someone wants to see you, they make room. Busy is an answer. Stop reading the messages. Start reading the calendar. Follow for more.

Tells in it:
- a triplet (memes / laughs / replies)
- "Here's the thing"
- "isn't X, it's Y"
- abstract nouns (*attention, interest, connection, effort*)
- an aphorism ("Busy is an answer")
- a parallel close ("Stop reading... Start reading...")
- no time, object or quote
- a lecture voice

**New spoken style** (82 words ≈ 34-36 s):

| s | Old (bookish) | New (spoken) |
|---|---|---|
| 0-3 | She sends you memes at one in the morning. | It's one fourteen a.m. She sends you a cat falling off a couch. |
| 3-7 | She laughs at your jokes. She replies within minutes. | Then another one. Then "bro this is literally you." |
| 7-13 | But when you ask her out, she's busy. | So next day you're like, "Drinks Friday?" And she goes... "ahh, this week's crazy for me." |
| 13-18 | Here's the thing: attention isn't the same as interest. | Crazy. She was up at one a.m. sending you cats. |
| 18-23 | Late-night texting is low-effort connection. It costs her nothing. | Look. I've been that guy. Three weeks of memes. Zero dates. |
| 23-26 | A date costs her time, and time is where real interest shows. | You're her one a.m. entertainment, man. |
| 26-32 | When someone wants to see you, they make room. Busy is an answer. | So ask once more. "Thursday or Saturday?" She dodges that too? Stop answering the memes. |
| 32-35 | Stop reading the messages. Start reading the calendar. Follow for more. | Follow for more. |

**New script, clean, as the TTS input** (Fish s2.1-pro, one line per chunk):

```
It's one fourteen a.m. She sends you a cat falling off a couch.
Then another one. Then, "bro, this is literally you."
So next day you're like, "Drinks Friday?"
And she goes... "ahh, this week's crazy for me."
[dry, amused] Crazy. She was up at one a.m. sending you cats.
Look. I've been that guy. Three weeks of memes. Zero dates.
You're her one a.m. entertainment, man.
[low, matter-of-fact] So ask once more. "Thursday or Saturday?"
She dodges that too? Stop answering the memes.
Follow for more.
```

On-screen text:
- 0-2 s: `MEMES AT 1AM`
- 7-13 s: `"this week's crazy 😩"` as a chat bubble
- 26-32 s: `THURSDAY OR SATURDAY?`

The slang ("LMAOOO 😭😭") lives in the on-screen chat bubbles, not in the voice.

Why it works:
- **Hook:** a scene with a time and an object.
- **Logic:** BUT turn at 7 s, SO move at 26 s.
- **Spoken features:** present tense, direct speech, fragments, one confession aside.
- **Clean of the ban list:** no abstract nouns, no "it's not X", no triplet, no aphorism.
- **Ending:** a copyable line and a concrete action.
- **CTA:** spoken, per the rule.
